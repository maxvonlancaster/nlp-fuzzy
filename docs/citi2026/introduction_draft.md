# Introduction (Draft)

Unsupervised extractive summarisation selects sentences from a source document without training data, which makes it attractive when labelled summaries are scarce or costly to produce. Graph-based methods such as TextRank and LexRank rank sentences by their centrality in a similarity graph, while algebraic methods such as latent semantic analysis (LSA) project sentences into a low-dimensional topic space and score them by their latent weight. These approaches remain widely used as baselines and as components of larger systems, and they are often compared on the basis of automatic evaluation metrics.

The most common metric for this comparison is ROUGE, which measures n-gram overlap between a system summary and one or more reference summaries. ROUGE is convenient and reproducible, but its rankings depend on the references that are used. When references are short, templated or written from a particular part of the source, a metric can reward methods whose selections happen to match that structure rather than methods that select the most informative content. Document structure compounds the problem: if a reference draws mainly on the opening of a document, a simple lead baseline may score highly regardless of the quality of more sophisticated selection strategies.

This concern is particularly relevant for clinical text. Medical transcriptions are long, heterogeneous and often organised in conventional sections, and the short descriptions attached to them are commonly used as proxy references. Such descriptions are not verified gold-standard summaries, so a comparison of methods on them measures agreement with a proxy rather than summary quality in an absolute sense. Recent unsupervised evaluations of this kind typically report aggregate scores and significance tests, but they seldom examine whether the reference itself carries a positional signal that could influence the ranking.

We therefore address the following gap: algorithm comparisons on unsupervised extractive summarisation are rarely accompanied by diagnostics of positional reference bias, and the robustness of any such diagnostic to document and reference length is seldom checked. Our objective is to compare five unsupervised extractive methods (Lead-3, Random, TextRank, LexRank and LSA) on a cleaned medical transcription corpus, and to assess whether the observed ranking is accompanied by a positional bias in the reference. We ask three research questions. First, how do the methods compare on ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L, and which pairwise differences survive multiple-comparison correction? Second, is the description's token overlap with the opening of a transcription associated with the advantage of Lead over LexRank? Third, does that association persist after controlling for document and reference length?

The contributions are fourfold. We report a five-method comparison of graph-based, algebraic and baseline extractive methods under a single, reproducible protocol. We provide a cleaned MTSamples evaluation corpus, obtained by removing records with missing descriptions and exact duplicate transcriptions, together with an audit that documents the redundancy present in the raw file. We apply paired statistical analysis with Holm correction, complemented by Wilcoxon tests, bootstrap confidence intervals and effect sizes, so that significance and practical magnitude are reported separately. Finally, we add positional-bias diagnostics with quartile analysis and length-controlled robustness checks.

The positional analysis is framed as an evaluation concern rather than a demonstrated mechanism. Its results describe associations in one dataset, and they do not establish that the reference was written from the opening text or that any method is superior in general.
