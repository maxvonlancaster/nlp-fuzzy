# Fuzzy and Frequency-Enhanced Forecasting for Financial Time Series

## Abstract

This study examines forecasting performance for a financial time series using a combination of fuzzy logic optimization and a frequency-enhanced transformer model. The objective was to evaluate whether a structured fuzzy rule system and a neural architecture designed to exploit recurring temporal structure can improve short-horizon forecasting under noisy market conditions. The notebook experiments transform the raw series into return-based targets, construct rolling windows, and compare several benchmarks against a fuzzy differential evolution model and a frequency-enhanced transformer model.

The empirical findings indicate that the transformer-based model achieved the strongest absolute-error performance in the reported benchmark comparison, with a mean squared error of 0.001305 and a root mean squared error of 0.036126, compared with a zero-return baseline and a naive persistence baseline. The fuzzy differential evolution model, while not competitive in the same absolute-error terms, produced a structured and interpretable output, with a mean absolute error of 0.065373 and a root mean squared error of 0.084019. The results suggest that the neural model is more effective at reducing forecast dispersion in the tested setting, whereas the fuzzy approach remains valuable for interpretability and uncertainty-aware rule construction. This work contributes to the ongoing discussion on hybrid forecasting strategies for nonstationary financial series and highlights the trade-off between predictive accuracy and model transparency.

## Keywords

Financial forecasting, fuzzy logic, differential evolution, transformer, time series, frequency enhancement, return modeling, nonstationary data

## Introduction

Forecasting financial time series remains a difficult problem because market data are affected by nonstationarity, regime changes, noise, nonlinear dependencies, and heterogeneous sources of information. Traditional statistical models often fail to capture long-range interactions and abrupt structural changes, while purely data-driven models can become unstable when the signal-to-noise ratio is weak. This challenge is particularly relevant for short-horizon prediction tasks, where small deviations in trend and volatility can materially affect decisions and interpretations.

The notebook underlying this article explores a specific response to this problem by combining two complementary modeling paradigms. The first is a fuzzy rule-based forecasting system optimized by differential evolution. The second is a frequency-enhanced transformer architecture designed to extract dominant periodic structure from temporal windows before forecasting future returns. The central motivation is not to claim that either model is universally superior, but to evaluate how optimization-driven fuzzy reasoning and transformer-based sequence modeling perform under the same forecasting conditions and to understand where each approach adds value.

The experimental design is intentionally conservative. Instead of using raw prices as the direct target, the workflow transforms the data into returns, which are often more stable for modeling relative changes. This transformation is justified by the fact that price series are typically dominated by scale and trend effects, whereas return dynamics more directly reflect the movement that is relevant to forecasting. The models are then compared against naive and zero-return benchmarks, allowing the evaluation to distinguish genuine predictive ability from trivial persistence or drift assumptions.

The main contribution of this work is the empirical comparison of two forecasting paradigms under a consistent preprocessing pipeline. In doing so, the study illustrates that absolute-error reduction and interpretability are not the same objective. The fuzzy differential evolution approach offers a transparent rule-based mechanism for encoding uncertainty, while the FEformer model focuses on predictive regularity and sequence structure. The reported outcomes suggest that the transformer architecture is more effective in the tested benchmark setting, but the fuzzy system still provides a meaningful interpretive layer for decision support in volatile environments.

## Methodological Framework

The forecasting problem is modeled as a supervised sequence prediction task in which a window of historical observations is used to forecast the next horizon of returns. The raw data are transformed according to the standard relative change definition

$$
 r_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}.
$$

This transformation is appropriate for financial data because it removes much of the scale heterogeneity present in absolute price levels and highlights the short-term movement relative to the prior state. It also reduces the risk that a model learns a nearly deterministic trend from price level rather than the actual predictive signal in returns.

The fuzzy differential evolution model implements a rule-based system with linguistic variables, fuzzy membership functions, and a rule base whose parameters are optimized through differential evolution. This choice is justified because fuzzy systems naturally capture approximate reasoning under uncertain or partially overlapping conditions, and differential evolution is well suited for global optimization in non-differentiable objective functions. In the notebook, the fuzzy procedure is designed to estimate future movement by combining membership strengths and optimized weights, rather than relying on a single deterministic mapping from the raw sequence to the target variable.

The neural component is a frequency-enhanced transformer, denoted here as FEformer. This architecture is selected because financial series often contain recurring patterns that are more visible after frequency decomposition, and the transformer mechanism is capable of modeling long-range dependencies across windows. The frequency enhancement is particularly relevant when the data include cyclical fluctuations or latent periodic components that are not easily captured by standard autoregressive or shallow machine learning baselines. In this notebook, the model receives a multivariate rolling window and produces a multi-horizon forecast, allowing the evaluation to focus on the next-step return path rather than only a single scalar output.

The evaluation procedure uses a train-test split and calculates a range of error measures: mean squared error, root mean squared error, mean absolute percentage error, symmetric mean absolute percentage error, mean absolute scaled error, and coefficient of determination. These metrics together address different aspects of forecast quality, including absolute error, relative error, scale normalization, and explanatory power. This is essential in financial forecasting, where no single metric is sufficient because extreme values, small denominators, and volatility spikes can distort simple comparisons.

## Experimental Setup

The experimental workflow in the notebook is designed to reflect a realistic forecasting setting. Historical observations are segmented into rolling windows, and the target variable is expressed in return form to align with the economic interpretation of short-term price movement. The train and test partitions are then used to evaluate out-of-sample forecast accuracy using the same set of benchmarks for each model category.

The fuzzy differential evolution system is benchmarked against the zero-return and naive assumptions. The zero-return benchmark represents a no-change expectation, while the naive benchmark repeats the last observed return or recent state as the forecast. These baselines are important because many weak forecasting systems can appear competitive when the data are noisy or highly persistent. By comparing against these reference strategies, the analysis is able to identify whether the learned model captures genuine structure rather than simply copying the latest observation.

The FEformer model is evaluated in the same framework. The model is measured on multi-horizon forecasts using the same return-based target, and the investigation reports horizon-specific metrics to assess whether predictive difficulty changes with the forecasting distance. The notebook also reconstructs price paths from the predicted returns to visualize actual and predicted prices in the original value domain. This is a valuable step because economic interpretation is often easier when the output is shown as a price trajectory rather than only as a return series.

## Results and Interpretation

The reported fuzzy differential evolution metrics indicate that the rule-based model is able to produce a structured forecast but underperforms the more flexible neural system in absolute-error terms. The final fuzzy model reports a mean absolute error of 0.065373, a mean squared error of 0.007059, and a root mean squared error of 0.084019. These values suggest that the model captures some directional signal, yet the forecast error remains materially higher than the transformer result. This outcome is not surprising given that fuzzy systems are highly interpretable but can be limited when the search space becomes large and the underlying dynamics are unstable.

The notebook also reports a MAPE of 962.127083 and a SMAPE of 142.612981, which are extremely large relative to typical forecasting studies. This is a consequence of the return-based target and the presence of low-value or near-zero denominators in the series. Such metrics should be interpreted carefully in financial contexts. A very small denominator can inflate percentage-based error, even when the absolute difference is modest in economic terms. This is one reason why the study uses multiple error metrics rather than relying on MAPE alone. The direction accuracy of 58.113208 indicates an improvement over random guessing but still leaves substantial room for better forecast calibration.

The FEformer results provide a clearer indication of predictive quality under the same evaluation protocol. The reported benchmark comparison lists the model with a mean squared error of 0.001305, a root mean squared error of 0.036126, a mean absolute scaled error of 0.637940, and a coefficient of determination of -0.025881. The value of MAPE is 140.127146 and SMAPE is 163.931513, which are again large in percentage terms, yet the absolute-error metrics remain materially smaller than those of the fuzzy differential evolution system. This pattern indicates that the frequency-enhanced transformer is more consistent in reducing variance and absolute deviations, even though percentage-based metrics remain sensitive to the volatility and sparsity of the underlying returns.

The overall benchmark comparison is informative because it places the neural model against the zero-return and naive alternatives. The zero benchmark is deliberately weak but useful as a lower bound. The naive benchmark is important because it checks whether the model can outperform the assumption that the recent state persists. The fact that the FEformer model reduces root mean squared error relative to those baselines demonstrates that the learned representation captures forecasting structure beyond trivial persistence. At the same time, the negative R-squared value warns against over-interpreting the improvement as strong explanatory power in a statistical sense. The notebook therefore communicates a measured conclusion: the architecture is competitive on absolute error, but the problem remains difficult and highly sensitive to market volatility.

The output also includes price-level visualizations reconstructed from the predicted returns. These plots are especially useful for interpretation because they show how the model’s prediction behaves when translated back into the original scale of prices. The visual comparison between actual and predicted price paths suggests that the model tracks broad movement patterns more than precise local turning points. This is consistent with the difficulty of short-term financial forecasting and with the fact that the process is shaped by both deterministic structures and stochastic shocks.

## Discussion

The results point to a clear methodological trade-off. The fuzzy differential evolution model is valuable because it is interpretable and because its rule structure can be inspected and adjusted. In financial decision support, interpretability matters: analysts often want to understand why a model expects a rise or a fall, not simply to obtain a numerical projection. The fuzzy system provides that type of reasoning, although its predictive error is higher in this study.

The FEformer model, by contrast, prioritizes predictive performance. Its architecture is designed to exploit temporal dependencies and dominant frequency components that may be obscured in raw sequences. This is a justified strategy for financial time series, where cyclical behavior and long-range dependency can carry predictive signal even when the raw values are noisy. The lower mean squared error and root mean squared error reported in the notebook are consistent with the hypothesis that frequency-aware transformer modeling is more effective for this dataset than purely rule-driven optimization in the tested configuration.

A second insight is that the evaluation metrics are not fully aligned. In financial forecasting, mean squared error and root mean squared error give a direct view of scale and dispersion, while percentage-based metrics can become unstable when the referenced values are small or fluctuate around zero. The notebook’s use of several metrics is therefore methodologically sound. It avoids the false reassurance that can come from a single summary statistic and demonstrates the practical challenge of comparing models on sequences with substantial volatility and near-zero movements.

This study also suggests important limitations. None of the models removes the inherent uncertainty of financial forecasting, and the negative coefficient of determination for the transformer indicates that there is still substantial unexplained variation in the data. The fuzzy model’s large percentage metrics highlight the same issue from another angle: rule-based systems can be interpretable but not necessarily robust under high volatility. These limitations are not weaknesses of the methodology alone; they reflect the fundamental difficulty of predicting short-term financial dynamics from noisy and weakly stationary observations.

## Conclusion

This article presents a comparative forecasting study based on a fuzzy differential evolution approach and a frequency-enhanced transformer model. The results show that the neural model achieved the lowest absolute-error metrics in the reported benchmark comparison, whereas the fuzzy system remains valuable for interpretability and structured reasoning. The methodological choice to model returns rather than absolute prices is justified by the need to focus on relative movement, which is the more relevant quantity in short-horizon financial forecasting.

The notebook also demonstrates the importance of evaluating models with multiple objective functions. The evidence does not support a purely one-sided conclusion that either approach is universally superior. Instead, the results suggest that model selection depends on the objective: if predictive error is the primary concern, the FEformer architecture is the stronger performer in the reported experiments; if explainability and domain-specific rule logic are required, the fuzzy differential evolution model remains a meaningful alternative.

In terms of future work, the most direct next step is to extend the comparative study across multiple assets and longer forecast horizons, while also examining regime-specific behavior in volatile periods. A further improvement would be to combine the representation power of transformer models with the interpretability of fuzzy logic in a single hybrid architecture, so that the forecasting system can preserve both accuracy and explanatory structure. Such a direction would respect the signal that emerges from this study: the strongest forecasting systems are not necessarily the most transparent, and the most transparent systems are not necessarily the most accurate, but both objectives can be pursued together when the modeling strategy is carefully matched to the nature of the data.

## Closing Note

The findings in this notebook are best understood as an empirical investigation rather than a definitive claim of market predictability. The evidence supports the view that frequency-aware neural forecasting can substantially reduce absolute forecast error under the tested conditions, while fuzzy differential evolution offers an interpretable complement for uncertainty-aware decision support. That balance between predictive performance and transparent reasoning is the core value of the study.
